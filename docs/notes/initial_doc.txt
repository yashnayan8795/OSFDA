ABSTRACT 

Commercial aviation safety has historically depended on the retrospective analysis of incidents 
through  static,  rule-based  monitoring  of  recorded  flight  data  and  the  manual  review  of 
voluntary incident narratives. While these approaches have fostered one of the safest modes of 
transport in human history, they are inherently reactive. Traditional systems detect only what 
they have been explicitly programmed to find, leaving subtle, multi-factor precursor patterns 
and  emerging  risk  themes  invisible  until  an  accumulation  of  incidents  finally  triggers 
regulatory or operator action. 

This  project  presents  an  integrated,  AI-driven  safety  and  telemetry  risk  analytics  platform 
designed to shift flight data monitoring from a reactive process to a proactive one, ultimately 
supporting aviation safety analysts at airlines and regulatory bodies. The system is built upon 
the  NASA  Aviation  Safety  Reporting  System  (ASRS)  corpus  of  38,655  voluntary  incident 
reports spanning two decades, joined with operational data from the Bureau of Transportation 
Statistics (BTS), the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB), and the National Oceanic 
and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA). To ensure trustworthy predictive performance, the 
framework is grounded in severe methodological discipline: a strict temporal split is applied to 
prevent future data leakage, and a rigorous audited whitelist ensures no post-incident variables 
influence pre-incident predictions. 

The  proposed  platform  integrates  five  interconnected  machine  learning  capabilities.  First,  a 
leakage-strict severity  triage model combines pre-circumstance tabular metadata with  PCA-
compressed Sentence-BERT narrative embeddings, applies an Ordinal LightGBM cumulative 
logit  decomposition,  calibrates  probabilities  through  temperature  scaling,  and  selects  the 
predicted class through cost-sensitive decision under an asymmetric cost matrix that penalises 
missed critical  incidents  one hundred times more heavily than minor over-triage. Second, a 
three-tier incident category classifier progresses from a TF-IDF baseline through a Sentence-
BERT text tower to a Fusion Multi-Layer Perceptron trained with focal loss, per-label positive-
class weighting, label smoothing, GELU activations, and BatchNorm under a cosine learning 
rate  schedule.  Third,  a  pre-flight  risk  scoring  methodology  combines  case-control  sampling 
with prior shift correction to produce deployment-ready calibrated probabilities, with a 200-
step  threshold  sweep  tuning  the  operational  decision  boundary.  Fourth,  an  unsupervised 
emerging-risk  discovery  pipeline  clusters  sentence  embeddings  and  applies  Pruned  Exact 
Linear Time (PELT) changepoint detection to flag structural shifts in risk themes before they 
escalate. Finally, a contributing-factor knowledge graph applies Louvain community detection 
and betweenness centrality analysis to identify bridge factors connecting disparate safety risks. 

These five capabilities are delivered through an interactive Streamlit analyst dashboard backed 
by  a  Python  FastAPI  inference  service  exposing  the  models  through  a  unified  GraphQL 
interface, with trained artifacts loaded into memory at startup. Software reliability is enforced 
via a Jenkins continuous integration pipeline requiring test passage and strict SonarQube static 
analysis quality gates at every code change. 

Under temporal validation, the severity triage model achieves a Quadratic Weighted Kappa of 
0.4612,  the  category  classifier  reaches  a  macro-F1  of  0.7038,  and  the  pre-flight  risk  model 
achieves  an  ROC-AUC  of  0.7641  on  the  held-out  test  set,  demonstrating  that  an  integrated 
machine learning system grounded in rigorous data hygiene can provide actionable proactive 
decision support without replacing the judgment of human safety experts.

1. INTRODUCTION 

1.1 BACKGROUND 

Commercial aviation is the safest form of long-distance transport ever engineered by humans. The 
accident rate for scheduled commercial flights has fallen by more than two orders of magnitude 
since  the  1960s,  despite  enormous  growth  in  the  global  fleet,  route  networks,  and  passenger 
volume. This safety record is the product of a deliberate, multi-layered safety culture combining 
rigorous  certification,  mandatory  incident  reporting,  structured  accident  investigations,  and 
continuous post-flight analysis of recorded operational data.  

Two practices form the operational backbone of modern airline safety analysis: Flight Operations 
Quality Assurance (FOQA) and Flight Data Monitoring (FDM). Every commercial flight produces 
a  stream  of  recorded  data  describing  aircraft  behaviour,  crew  actions,  and  environmental 
conditions. Patterns in this data, analysed across thousands of flights, reveal precursors to accidents 
that no single flight crew or maintenance  event  could  detect  alone. Airlines feed this data into 
FOQA programs to flag operational exceedances, such as unstable approaches or hard landings. 
Concurrently,  voluntary  reporting  programs  like  the  NASA  Aviation  Safety  Reporting  System 
(ASRS) capture the human factors of operational events through narratives submitted by pilots 
and  controllers,  while  bodies  like  the  National  Transportation  Safety  Board  (NTSB)  maintain 
authoritative accident records.  

However, these mature programs share a common limitation: the analysis they enable is mostly 
retrospective and rule based. FOQA exceedances rely on static thresholds, successfully flagging 
specific  events  but  missing  subtle  precursor  patterns  that  combine  conditions  which  are 
individually  within  tolerance  but  jointly  indicate  elevated  risk.  Furthermore,  new  risk  themes 
remain invisible until accumulated incident reports force regulatory action, often years after the 
underlying risk first appeared. Additionally, FOQA data, voluntary incident narratives, accident 
records, and weather observations are collected and analysed in disconnected pipelines, severely 
limiting proactive investigations.  

1.1.1 Existing Solutions and Their Limitations 

Many commercial and academic systems address parts of the safety analytics problem, but none 
integrate the full range of capabilities required for proactive analysis.  

•  Commercial  FOQA  Platforms:  Mature  and  well-validated  for  structured  exceedance 

monitoring, but they remain almost entirely rule-based and proprietary.  

•  Open-Source  Tools:  Focus  primarily  on  text  mining  of  narrative  reports  but  rarely 

integrate structured flight data or pre-flight risk modelling.  

•  Generic Large Language Models (LLMs): Can answer general aviation questions but 
fail  to  produce  structured  analytic  outputs,  perform  strict  severity  triage,  or  generate 
calibrated risk probabilities grounded in real incident data.  

•  Data  Leakage  Vulnerabilities:  A  common  pitfall  in  incident  data  modelling  is  the 
unintended  inclusion  of  post-incident  outcome  fields  among  the  predictors.  Models 
predicting  severity  from  features  that  already  contain  documented  outcomes  produce 
impressive  metrics  that  instantly  collapse  when  deployed  against  new  flights  where 
outcomes are unknown. Few publicly described systems take an explicit, audited stance on 
preventing this leakage.

1.2 MOTIVATION 

This project is driven by a specific, highly visible gap in the public aviation safety data ecosystem, 
focusing on three main aspects:  

•  Problem Significance: The raw material for proactive aviation safety analytics is publicly 
available at an unprecedented scale, including tens of thousands of NASA ASRS narrative 
reports,  Bureau  of  Transportation  Statistics  (BTS)  on-time  performance  data,  NTSB 
accident  databases,  and  National  Oceanic  and  Atmospheric  Administration  (NOAA) 
weather observations. What is missing is an integrated, methodologically rigorous platform 
that joins these sources and surfaces actionable patterns to analysts.  

•  Practical and Academic Motivation: Safety analysts currently rely on a patchwork of vendor 
tools and custom scripts, slowing  investigation and fragmenting  knowledge. Integrating 
advanced machine learning techniques into a single platform under shared methodological 
discipline represents a vital academic and practical step for safety-critical domains.  
•  Technical Interest and Innovation: The project addresses unresolved technical challenges 
by implementing a strict whitelist-based leakage audit, a cost-sensitive ordinal prediction 
model penalizing fatal misclassifications 100 times more heavily than minor over-triage, a 
case-control  sampling  design  with  prior  shift  correction  for  rare  events,  and  severity-
weighted topic risk scoring.  

1.3 SCOPE OF THE PROJECT 

The proposed AI-driven safety and telemetry risk analytics platform functions as a comprehensive 
decision support tool for aviation safety analysts at airlines and regulatory bodies.  

1.3.1 Functional Scope 

The system accepts structured incident metadata, narrative text, and flight-level operational data 
to produce five classes of analytic output:  

2 

 
 
 
 
1.  Severity  Triage:  Estimates  likely  severity  on  a  four-level  ordinal  scale  using  only  pre-
circumstance  metadata,  returning  calibrated  probabilities  and  a  cost-sensitive  predicted 
class minimizing expected misclassification cost.  

2.  Incident  Category  Classification:  Assigns  safety  categories  to  reports  using  a  three-tier 

architecture (TF-IDF, SBERT text tower, and a Fusion Multi-Layer Perceptron).  

3.  Pre-Flight Risk Scoring: Produces a calibrated probability of an incident  occurring  on  a 
planned flight based on route, time, aircraft, carrier, and forecast weather, handling rare-
event base rates via case-control sampling and prior shift correction.  

4.  Emerging Risk Discovery: Identifies emerging risk topics from recent narrative reports by 
clustering narratives, tracking  prevalence, computing severity-weighted risk scores, and 
flagging structural changepoints.  

5.  Contributing Factor Graph Analysis: Constructs a knowledge graph of contributing factors, 
flight  phases,  aircraft  types,  and  FAR  parts,  using  Louvain  community  detection  and 
betweenness centrality to surface risk clusters and bridge factors.  

1.3.2 Non-Functional Scope 

The  system  supports  sub-second  response  times  for  inference  operations  and  multi-second 
response  times  for  graph  and  emerging  risk  panels.  It  features  a  modular  architecture  where 
individual models can be retrained independently. The platform is built for portable deployment, 
with  the  entire  data  and  model  pipeline  reproducible  from  a  fixed  global  seed.  Continuous 
integration (CI) enforces test passage and static analysis quality gates before any code changes are 
accepted.  

1.3.3 Technical Scope and Project Boundaries 

The  frontend  utilizes  a  Streamlit-based  interactive  dashboard  backed  by  a  Python  FastAPI 
inference service via a GraphQL interface. Continuous integration is managed through Jenkins, 
with code quality enforced by SonarQube. Machine learning components are implemented using 
LightGBM,  CatBoost,  scikit-learn,  PyTorch,  Sentence-Transformers,  BERTopic,  ruptures,  and 
NetworkX. Models are trained offline using 38,655 NASA ASRS voluntary incident reports joined 
with BTS, NTSB, and NOAA data.  

Limitations: The platform processes structured records and narrative text but does not consume 
real-time avionics data, live flight telemetry, images, or audio. It serves exclusively as a decision 
support  tool,  not  a  flight-critical  system;  it  does  not  interface  directly  with  aircraft  systems, 
produce certified outputs (e.g., DO-178C or ARP4754A), or replace human judgment. Outputs are 
designed as starting points for human investigation rather than final automated decisions.

2. PROJECT DESCRIPTION AND GOALS 

2.1 LITERATURE REVIEW 

The application of computational analytics to aviation safety has evolved significantly over the 
past  two  decades,  transitioning  from  static,  rule-based  systems  to  advanced  deep  learning  and 
natural language processing (NLP) architectures. The literature and patent landscape relevant to 
the proposed platform can be categorized into four primary domains: pre-flight risk assessment, 
incident severity classification, multi-label narrative categorization, and unsupervised emerging-
risk discovery. 

2.1.1 Pre-Flight Risk Assessment and Telemetry Analytics 

Early approaches  to  flight risk quantification  relied heavily on deterministic rulesets  and static 
database cross-referencing. Adkins established a foundational precedent by patenting a pre-flight 
risk measuring system that combined static accident-history databases with non-static weather and 
traffic databases to trigger alerts on a flight-deck computer. While this established the legal and 
operational framework for pre-departure scoring derived from heterogeneous databases, it lacked 
the ability to learn complex, non-linear precursor patterns.  

More recently, commercial entities have explored machine learning for flight-level risk scoring, 
primarily for operational and financial optimization. Swiss Reinsurance Company Ltd. patented a 
system predicting flight trajectory deviations and delay risks, utilizing historical phase tables to 
automate  risk-transfer  pipelines.  This  confirmed  the  commercial  viability  of  ML-driven  flight 
scoring  but  focused  strictly  on  delay  rather  than  safety  severity.  Furthermore,  Honeywell 
International  Inc.  introduced  a  qualitative  risk  assessment  method  for  aircraft,  rendering  risk 
values derived from equipment state, crew factors, and environmental context directly onto avionic 
displays. However, these systems fundamentally rely on qualitative or rule-driven heuristic scoring 
rather  than  calibrated,  data-driven  probabilities  derived  from  case-control  matched  incident 
corpora.  

2.1.2 Aviation Incident Severity and Risk Classification 

The  challenge  of  classifying  the  severity  of  an  aviation  incident  using  post-flight  reports  has 
garnered  significant  academic  interest.  Zhang  and  Mahadevan  proposed  an  ensemble  machine 
learning model for aviation incident risk prediction, employing a hybrid Support Vector Machine 
(SVM) and Deep Neural Network (DNN) architecture. Their work demonstrated the efficacy of 
fusing narrative text with contextual, structured features to classify reports into five distinct risk 
levels.  

Building  upon  this,  Wang  et  al.  developed  a  fusion  model  combining  Convolutional  Neural 
Networks  (CNNs)  for  structured  data  and  Bidirectional  Long  Short-Term  Memory  (BiLSTM) 
networks  for  narrative  text.  This  architecture  successfully  integrated  multiple  modalities  to 
identify risk levels. However, a persistent limitation in these studies is the implicit vulnerability to 
data leakage. The feature sets often incorporate post-incident outcomes (e.g., component failures, 
damage assessments) to predict the severity of the event, generating artificially high-performance 
metrics that do not reflect true predictive capability against new, uncompleted flights.

2.1.3 Narrative Classification and Natural Language Processing 

The  NASA  Aviation  Safety  Reporting  System  (ASRS)  contains  a  rich  corpus  of  unstructured 
narrative  text,  prompting  extensive  research  into  automated  categorization.  Early  approaches 
utilized  weakly  supervised  learning.  Zhao  and  Ng  applied  a  semantic  lexicon  framework  to 
identify  causal  factors  in  ASRS  reports,  treating  the  task  as  a  multi-label  text  classification 
problem. This validated that ASRS narratives contain sufficient signal for causal factor extraction, 
though it relied heavily on rigid lexical constraints.  

The  advent  of  transformer-based  architectures  revolutionized  this  domain.  Kierszbaum  and 
Lapasset applied a distilled BERT model for question-answering on ASRS reports, demonstrating 
that  transformer  language  models  could  successfully  extract  structured  semantic  answers  from 
domain-specific  free  text.  Chandra  et  al.  significantly  advanced  this  by  developing  "Aviation-
BERT,"  a  domain-adaptive  pre-trained  model  fine-tuned  on  over  540,000  NTSB  and  ASRS 
narratives. Their work proved that domain-specific pre-training substantially outperforms generic 
models (like BERT-base-uncased) on downstream aviation classification tasks. 

2.1.4 Topic Modeling and Emerging Risk Discovery 

Unsupervised discovery of latent safety themes is critical for proactive hazard mitigation. Kuhn 
applied Structural Topic Modeling (STM) to ASRS near-miss reports, proving that probabilistic 
topic modeling could surface latent themes and temporal trends without predefined taxonomies.  

Recent advancements have transitioned from STM to embedding-based clustering. Mumtaz et al. 
proposed  a  domain-adapted  BERTopic  framework  (SafeCivAirBERT)  to  mine  and  track  the 
temporal  evolution  of  civil  aircraft  operational  risk  factors.  Their  research  demonstrated  that 
coupling  domain-adaptive  pre-training  with  BERTopic  yields  highly  coherent,  stable,  and 
interpretable topics. Nevertheless, prior art largely treats topic modeling as an isolated analytical 
exercise,  rarely  integrating  it  with  automated,  real-time  changepoint  detection  or  severity-
weighted scoring systems designed for immediate analyst triage.  

2.2 RESEARCH GAP 

While  the  literature  and  patent  landscape  demonstrate  substantial  progress  in  isolated  aviation 
machine learning tasks, a comprehensive review reveals four critical research and methodological 
gaps that the proposed platform addresses: 

1.  Methodological Indiscipline and Data Leakage: The most severe gap in existing severity 
prediction  literature  is  the  lack  of  a  strict,  audited  boundary  between  pre-circumstance 
predictors and post-incident outcomes. Models frequently learn to predict severity using 
features  that  describe  the  incident's  aftermath  (e.g.,  "aircraft  destroyed"  or  "emergency 
declared"). There is a profound need for a system built upon a strict feature whitelist that 
evaluates true predictive capacity using solely pre-incident metadata.  

2.  Absence  of  Calibration  and  Cost-Sensitive  Evaluation:  Existing  systems  largely 
evaluate  classification  performance  using  standard  accuracy  or  F1  scores.  In  aviation 
safety, missing a critical incident is orders of magnitude more dangerous than over-triaging 
a  minor  one.  Current  research  fails  to  implement  asymmetric  cost  matrices  and  rarely 
subjects raw model logits to isotonic or Platt calibration, which is mandatory if predictions 
are to be used for operational threshold-based alerting.  

3.  Positive-Unlabeled Identifiability in Pre-Flight Risk: Models predicting the likelihood 
of an incident on a future flight often fail to account for the extreme class imbalance (the

capabilities—alongside graph-based causal analysis—into a unified inference service with 
strict temporal data splitting and explicit model provenance for end-user analysts.  

2.3 OBJECTIVES 

The primary aim of this project is to deliver an integrated, reproducible aviation-safety analytics 
platform  that  addresses  the  identified  research  gaps  through  a  shared  data  pipeline,  a  unified 
inference  service,  and  a  single  analyst  dashboard.  To  achieve  this  aim,  the  following  specific 
objectives have been established: 

•  To curate and integrate disparate public aviation datasets—including NASA ASRS, BTS 
on-time performance, NTSB accident records, and NOAA METAR weather data—into a 
single reproducible, temporally split (train/validation/test) corpus. 

•  To design a deterministic severity rubric and a multi-label category taxonomy that engineer 
supervised  learning  targets  from  structured  ASRS  fields  without  relying  on  subjective 
human re-labelling. 

•  To implement and enforce a strict, version-controlled feature whitelist that prevents post-
incident  outcome  fields  from  leaking  into  predictive  models,  validated  automatically  at 
every continuous integration build. 

•  To  develop,  calibrate,  and  evaluate  five  distinct  machine  learning  pipelines:  a  cost-
sensitive gradient-boosted severity classifier, a three-tier multi-modal category classifier, 
a prior-shift corrected pre-flight risk scorer, a BERTopic-based emerging-risk discovery 
pipeline, and a contributing-factor knowledge graph. 

•  To  architect  a  unified  inference  backend  using  FastAPI  and  GraphQL,  serving  all  five 
analytic  capabilities  with  explicit  model-status  provenance  (e.g.,  TRAINED,  STUB, 
UNAVAILABLE) attached to every prediction. 

•  To  design  and  deploy  an  interactive,  front-end  dashboard  that  allows  safety  analysts  to 
explore live predictions, conduct "what-if" explorations, and visualize complex networks 
and temporal changepoints. 

•  To  package  the  entire  platform  as  containerized  microservices,  supported  by  a  robust 
Continuous Integration (CI) pipeline that enforces test passage, code-style consistency, and 
static-analysis quality gates on every code commit. 

2.4 PROBLEM STATEMENT 

Despite the abundance of high-quality aviation safety data—including the NASA Aviation Safety 
Reporting  System  (ASRS),  NTSB  accident  records,  BTS  flight  data,  and  NOAA  weather 
telemetry—current analytic capabilities remain highly fragmented and reactive. Specifically:  

•  Severity triage of incoming incident reports is largely a manual, labor-intensive process.  
•  Incident categorization relies on subjective human assessment against internal taxonomies.  
•  Pre-flight  risk  reasoning  is  typically  operator-specific  and  rule-based  rather  than  driven  by 

calibrated probabilities.  

6 

 
 
 
 
 
•  The  discovery  of  emerging  risk  themes  is  reactive,  typically  occurring  only  after  post-hoc 

regulatory action.  

•  Contributing-factor analysis is relegated to static spreadsheets, obscuring complex systemic 

failures.  

The  Exact  Problem:  There  is  an  absence  of  a  single,  methodologically  disciplined  machine 
learning  platform  that  integrates  these  fragmented  capabilities  while  strictly  enforcing  data 
hygiene—specifically  leakage  prevention,  temporal  validation,  and  probability  calibration.  To 
solve this, the project decomposes the analytics gap into five specific sub-problems:  

1.  Severity  Triage:  Predicting  incident  severity  using  only  pre-circumstance  metadata  to 

prevent data leakage.  

2.  Category  Classification:  Assigning  multi-label  safety  categories  by  fusing  structured 

metadata with free-text narratives.  

3.  Pre-Flight Risk Scoring: Estimating the calibrated probability of an incident for a planned 
flight, utilizing case-control sampling and prior-shift correction to handle rare-event base 
rates.  

4.  Emerging-Risk  Discovery:  Identifying  shifting  narrative  themes  using  unsupervised 

clustering and changepoint detection.  

5.  Contributing-Factor Graph: Constructing a knowledge graph to identify highly severe, 

recurring edge patterns and systemic bridge factors.

3. TECHNICAL SPECIFICATION 

3.1 REQUIREMENTS 

This section captures the functional and non-functional requirements that the platform was built 
to  satisfy.  The  functional  requirements  describe  what  the  system  does.  The  non-functional 
requirements  describe  the  operational  qualities  such  as  performance,  scalability,  security, 
reliability, and portability that the system was designed to meet. 

3.1.1 Functional Requirements 

•  The  system  must  accept  aviation  incident  metadata,  narrative  text,  and  flight-level 
operational  data  as  input  to  produce  analytic  outputs  across  five  interconnected  risk 
problems.  

•  The system must classify incident severity on a four-level ordinal scale (minor, moderate, 
substantial, and critical) using only pre-circumstance metadata, ensuring all post-incident 
outcome fields are excluded via an audited feature whitelist.  

•  Severity  prediction  must  return  calibrated  probabilities  for  each  level,  utilizing  a  cost-
sensitive  predicted  class  chosen  to  minimize  expected  misclassification  cost  under  a 
configured asymmetric cost matrix.  

•  The  system  must  classify  incident  narratives  into  one  or  more  of  five  safety  categories 
(flight  operations,  equipment  and  systems,  ATC  and  communication,  environment,  and 
airspace and navigation) using a three-tier architecture.  

•  This  category  classification must progress  from  a TF-IDF baseline through a Sentence-
BERT  text  tower  to  a  Fusion  Multi-Layer  Perceptron,  supporting  per-label  decision 
threshold tuning on a validation set.  

•  The system must provide a pre-flight risk scoring methodology joining flight-level on-time 

performance data with weather observations and historical incident records.  

•  Pre-flight risk scoring must support case-control sampling to handle rare-event base rates, 
utilizing  prior-shift  correction  and  isotonic  regression  to  recover  deployment-ready 
probability estimates.  

•  The system must perform unsupervised emerging-risk discovery on narrative corpora by 
clustering texts into topics, tracking topic prevalence over time, and computing a severity-
weighted risk score.  

•  Emerging-risk discovery must detect structural changepoints in topic time series utilizing 
the  Pruned  Exact  Linear  Time  (PELT)  algorithm  and  rank  top  risks  for  direct  analyst 
review.  

•  The  system  must  construct  a  contributing-factor  knowledge  graph  linking  contributing 
factors, flight phases, aircraft type families, and Federal Aviation Regulations (FAR) parts, 
with edges weighted by co-occurrence frequency.  

•  The knowledge graph must apply Louvain community detection to identify risk clusters 
and  betweenness  centrality  to  identify  bridge  factors  connecting  otherwise  separate 
clusters.  

•  The system must expose all five capabilities through a unified backend GraphQL interface, 
powering  a  multi-panel  interactive  dashboard  that  supports  visual  explorations  like 
confusion matrices, trend overlays, and interactive network graphs.  

9 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
•  The  system  must  guarantee  full  reproducibility,  ensuring  every  model  artifact  and 
processed dataset can be regenerated from raw data through deterministic scripts utilizing 
a fixed global seed.  

3.1.2 Non-Functional Requirements 

•  Performance:  The  platform  must  support  analyst-level  interaction  with  sub-second 
response  times  for  severity,  category,  and  pre-flight  risk  inference,  and  multi-second 
response times for larger graph and emerging-risk payloads. Model artifacts must load into 
memory once at service startup to eliminate prediction latency.  

•  Scalability:  The  modular  architecture  must  allow  individual  models  to  be  replaced  or 
retrained independently without disrupting other endpoints. Heavy offline processing (e.g., 
topic  modeling  and  embedding  generation)  must  run  separately  from  the  live  inference 
service.  

•  Security: Backend endpoints must be protected against malformed inputs via GraphQL 
schema  validation,  and  Cross-Origin  Resource  Sharing  (CORS)  must  be  configured  to 
strictly allow only the dashboard origin.  

•  Reliability: The data pipeline must fail loudly during offline processing if leakage audits, 
target  engineering  rules,  or  temporal  split  constraints  are  violated.  The  system  must 
degrade  gracefully  (e.g.,  falling  back 
to  Principal  Component  Analysis  and 
MiniBatchKMeans  if  primary  clustering  dependencies  fail  to  load)  without  breaking 
downstream logic.  

•  Portability: The system must be fully  containerized for deployment  on standard Linux 
environments,  while  also  running  seamlessly  on  macOS  and  Windows  development 
machines, utilizing YAML files for all human-readable configurations.  

•  Maintainability: Continuous integration (CI) must enforce that every code change passes 
an extensive test suite and static-analysis quality gates (covering code style, complexity, 
security, and coverage) before merging.  

3.2 FEASIBILITY STUDY 

3.2.1 Technical Feasibility 

The platform is technically feasible because it is built almost entirely on mature, widely supported, 
open source technologies. The Python  ecosystem  provides production grade libraries  for  every 
stage  of  the  pipeline.  LightGBM  and  CatBoost  are  battle  tested  gradient  boosted  decision  tree 
implementations. Scikit-learn provides the isotonic calibration, baseline classifiers, and evaluation 
metrics. Sentence-Transformers provides pre trained sentence embedding models that run on CPU, 
so no specialised AI hardware is needed. PyTorch supports the Fusion MLP model with standard 
CPU  based  training  that  completes  in  minutes  on  a  development  machine.  BERTopic,  with  its 
underlying pluggable architecture for dimensionality  reduction and clustering, supports the fall 
back  to  PCA  and  MiniBatchKMeans  on  platforms  where  the  Numba  based  dependencies  are 
unavailable. The ruptures library implements the PELT changepoint detection algorithm in pure 
Python with NumPy. NetworkX provides the graph data structures, Louvain community detection, 
and centrality computations. 

FastAPI  with  the  Strawberry  GraphQL  library  provides  a  modern,  asynchronous  backend 
framework with automatic schema validation and interactive query exploration. Streamlit provides 
a rapid UI development framework that is well suited for analyst facing data exploration interfaces. 
Jenkins  and  SonarQube  together  provide  continuous  integration  and  code  quality  enforcement 
using technologies that are standard in industrial software development.

4. SYSTEM DESIGN 

4.1 SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE 

The  platform  utilizes  a  layered  and  modular  architecture  divided  into  five  logical  layers:  the 
presentation layer, the API gateway layer, the model inference layer, the offline training and data 
pipeline layer, and the data and artifact storage layer. A separate continuous integration (CI) and 
quality enforcement pipeline cuts across all layers. This design keeps each component focused on 
a single responsibility, enables isolated testing, and allows the offline training pipeline to evolve 
independently of the live inference service.

4.1.1 Presentation Layer 

Built using Streamlit, this layer provides a multi-page interactive dashboard tailored for analyst-

4.1.2 API Gateway Layer 

Implemented  using  Python  FastAPI  and  the  Strawberry  GraphQL  library,  a  single  GraphQL

REST because it allows the dashboard to fetch the exact combination of model metadata, statistics, 
and  prediction  results  required  for  a  given  screen  in  a  single  request.  Cross-Origin  Resource

4.1.3 Model Inference Layer 

This  layer holds the trained model artifacts  and  prediction  logic for each of the five problems. 
Inference services share a singleton model store that loads artifacts once at startup and holds them 
in memory for the lifetime of the process. Depending on the request, this layer applies the trained 
models, executes post-processing (e.g., isotonic calibration, cost-sensitive class assignment), or 
reads precomputed analytic outputs from the offline pipeline.  

4.1.4 Offline Training and Data Pipeline Layer 

Operating  independently  of  the  inference  service,  this  layer  transforms  raw  input  datasets  into 
trained model artifacts and precomputed analytic outputs. The pipeline is structured as a sequence 
of deterministic phase scripts focusing on data foundation, severity model training, category model 
training, emerging risk discovery, and factor graph construction.  

4.1.5 Data and Artifact Storage Layer 

This filesystem-based layer holds three categories of files: raw input data (Apache Parquet format), 
processed analytic outputs (Parquet, JSON, CSV), and trained model artifacts (CatBoost binaries, 
LightGBM  text  files,  joblib  bundles,  and  serialized  BERTopic  models).  Each  artifact  is 
accompanied by a JSON metadata file containing versioning, training timestamps, and primary 
metric values.  

4.1.6 Continuous Integration and Quality Enforcement 

A CI pipeline, managed via Jenkins and SonarQube, runs across all layers. Every code change 
triggers a build that executes an 86-test suite and static analysis quality gates (covering code style, 
complexity,  security,  and  coverage).  This  ensures  methodological  invariants,  such  as  leakage 
prevention, are enforced at every commit.

4.3 MACHINE LEARNING MODEL DESIGNS 

4.3.1 Severity Triage Model Design (Problem A) 

The  severity  triage  capability  is  four-level  ordinal  classification  problem  mapping  incident 
outcomes  to  minor,  moderate,  substantial,  and  critical  levels.  Predictors  are  restricted  to  pre-
circumstance  information  only  (e.g.,  temporal  context,  geographic  context,  environmental 
conditions,  aircraft  and  crew  metadata)  via  an  audited  feature  whitelist.  The  deployed  model 
utilizes  a  CatBoost  gradient-boosted  classifier  featuring  categorical  handling,  balanced  class 
weighting,  and  per-class  isotonic  calibration.  A  custom  cost  matrix  enforces  cost-sensitive 
prediction, penalizing the misclassification of a critical incident as minor 100 times more heavily 
than over-triaging a minor incident as critical.  

4.3.2 Three-Tier Category Classification Design (Problem B) 

Incident  categorization  utilizes  a  three-tier  architecture  allowing  transparent  performance 
attribution:  

1.  Tier 1 (TF-IDF Baseline): A sparse bag-of-words representation paired with a one-vs-rest 

logistic regression classifier.  

2.  Tier  2  (SBERT  Text  Tower):  A  384-dimensional  Sentence-BERT  dense  embedding 

paired with logistic regression.  

20 

 
 
 
 
3.  Tier  3  (Fusion  MLP):  Combines  SBERT  text  embeddings  with  LightGBM  leaf-index 
embeddings (capturing tabular features), passing the concatenated representation through 
a PyTorch multi-layer perceptron with per-category sigmoid heads.  

FIGURE 4.11— Three-Tier Category Model Architecture 

Description:  Top-to-bottom  diagram  detailing  the  three  horizontal  tiers,  isolating  the 
contributions of contextual embeddings and text-tabular fusion. 

4.3.3 Pre-Flight Risk Modelling Design (Problem C) 

Addressing the extreme class imbalance of incident prediction (where the base rate is well below 
5%), this model employs a case-control sampling design. NTSB accident records are matched to 
BTS non-incident flights by route and month. Post-training, prior-shift correction is applied using 
a closed-form odds adjustment formula to recover deployment-ready probabilities that accurately 
reflect the true population base rate, followed by isotonic calibration.  

21 

 
 
 
 
4.3.4 Emerging Risk Discovery Design (Problem D) 

This unsupervised pipeline clusters narrative reports using Sentence-BERT embeddings reduced 
via  Principal  Component  Analysis  (PCA)  and  MiniBatchKMeans.  The  BERTopic  framework 
groups these into 30 human-readable topics. A severity-weighted risk score is computed for each 
topic by combining its temporal growth rate with the mean severity of its incidents. Furthermore, 
the Pruned Exact Linear Time (PELT) algorithm detects structural changepoints in the monthly 
count time series of each topic, flagging definitive shifts over routine fluctuations.  

4.3.5 Contributing Factor Knowledge Graph Design (Problem E) 

A heterogeneous co-occurrence graph is constructed from four node types: contributing factors, 
flight  phases,  aircraft  type  families,  and  FAR  parts.  Edges  are  weighted  by  co-occurrence 
frequency  within  the  same  incident  and  carry  the  mean  severity  of  the  contributing  incidents. 
Louvain  community  detection  identifies  densely  connected  clusters  of  risk,  while  betweenness 
centrality identifies bridge factors that connect isolated sub-graphs, surfacing highly frequent and 
serious factor combinations for analyst review.

5. METHODOLOGY AND TESTING 

5.1 SYSTEM DEVELOPMENT METHODOLOGY 

The development methodology adopted for the proposed platform combines two complementary 
frameworks. The Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe) governs the iterative delivery cadence and the 
prioritization of work. The V-Model governs the verification and validation discipline applied to 
each piece of work as it moves from requirements to deployed artifacts. The combination is highly 
appropriate  for  a  safety-adjacent  system  where  iteration  speed  matters,  but  correctness  matters 
more. 

5.1.1 Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe) 

The Scaled Agile Framework structures the work of the project into a cadence of short iterations 
grouped into longer planning increments. Each iteration is two weeks long and produces a working 
increment  of  the  system.  A  planning  increment  spans  five  iterations,  at  the  end  of  which  the 
cumulative output is reviewed against the planned outcomes for that increment, and priorities for 
the next increment are set. 

The work itself is organized into a four-level hierarchy: 

•  Themes describe the long-term direction of the project (e.g., "build an integrated aviation 

safety analytics platform"). 

•  Epics  decompose  themes  into  multi-increment  bodies  of  work  (e.g.,  "implement  the 

severity triage capability end to end"). 

•  Features decompose epics into single-increment bodies of work (e.g., "train and calibrate 

the CatBoost severity classifier"). 

•  Stories decompose features into single-iteration units of work that can be completed and 
verified within two weeks (e.g., "fit per-class isotonic calibrators on the validation set and 
persist them as a joblib bundle"). 

Each iteration begins with a planning event in which selected stories are reviewed, estimated, and 
committed. The iteration concludes with a review and retrospective. This cadence proved valuable 
because  the  project  spans  five  distinct  machine  learning  problems  with  substantially  different 
methodological  requirements;  the  iteration  cadence  allowed  each  problem  to  advance 
independently  without  blocking  others,  while  the  planning  increment  review  ensured  the 
integrated platform remained coherent. 

5.1.2 V-Model Verification and Validation Lifecycle 

The V-Model complements the iterative cadence of SAFe by enforcing that every artifact produced 
has a corresponding verification or validation activity. The model is conventionally drawn as a V-
shape,  with  design  and  development  activities  descending  the  left  arm  and  corresponding 
verification and validation activities ascending the right arm. Each level of design has a paired test 
activity at the same level, ensuring that no piece of work is considered complete until validated 
against its motivating requirement. 

23 

 
 
 
 
 
FIGURE 5.1 — V-Model Lifecycle Adopted in System Development 

The  V-Model  levels  were  instantiated  as  follows:  Functional  and  non-functional  requirements 
(Chapter 3) were paired with acceptance testing performed against the analyst dashboard. System 
architecture  and  component  design  (Chapter  4)  were  paired  with  system-level  integration  tests 
exercising the full FastAPI inference service. Detailed design of each ML problem was paired with 
integration tests exercising models end-to-end through the offline pipeline. Implementation was 
paired with unit tests at the function and class level. Continuous integration enforced that every 
code change satisfied every level before being accepted into the main branch. 

5.1.3 How SAFe and V-Model Combine in This Project 

The  two  frameworks  operate  at  different  scales  and  address  different  concerns.  SAFe  answers 
"what should we work on next, and how do we keep the team aligned." The V-Model answers 
"how  do  we  know  it  is  correct  before  we  ship  it."  They  combine  cleanly  because  every  story 
committed in a SAFe iteration must satisfy the V-Model levels relevant to its scope before it can 
be marked done. For instance, a story to add a new feature to the severity model must include unit 
tests, integration tests, and an update to the leakage audit.

5.2.1 Public Aviation Safety Data Sources 

The project draws on four publicly available aviation safety data sources:

The  data  ingestion  pipeline  transforms  raw  data  into  a  labeled,  feature-engineered,  temporally

5.2.3 Target Engineering: Severity Rubric and Category Taxonomy 

Targets are derived via two deterministic rule sets. The severity rubric is calibrated against NTSB 
definitions (49 CFR Part 830), FAA separation categories (Order 7210.56), and ICAO accident 
criteria. It maps outcomes to four levels: minor (0), moderate (1), substantial (2), and critical (3).

This deterministic mapping is methodologically critical to avoid circular reasoning traps observed 
in prior literature.  

The category taxonomy maps the Assessments_Contributing_Factors_and_Situations field into five non-
exclusive  categories:  Flight  Operations,  Equipment  and  Systems,  ATC  and  Communication, 
Environment, and Airspace and Navigation.  

5.2.4 Leakage Audit Methodology 

The leakage audit ensures no field describing an incident's outcome reaches the feature set of a 
model  attempting  to  predict  that  outcome—a  severe  methodological  flaw  present  in  several 
existing  models.  The  audit  operates  through  a  version-controlled  YAML  whitelist.  Every 
admissible feature requires a written justification. Post-incident fields (e.g., event anomalies, miss 
distance) are strictly excluded from the pre-circumstance severity model. This audit is enforced at 
pipeline run-time and via a pytest assertion at code-commit time.  

5.2.5 Temporal Train, Validation, and Test Split 

The dataset is partitioned by date to prevent future data leakage. The training set covers 2012–
2018,  the  validation  set  covers  2018–2019  (used  for  early  stopping,  isotonic  calibration,  and 
threshold tuning), and the test set covers 2019–2022. 

5.3 FEATURE ENGINEERING METHODOLOGY 

5.3.1 Temporal Feature Derivation 

The raw date field is parsed into ordinal features (year, month, quarter). Local time is bucketed 
into morning, afternoon, evening, and night. Cyclical encoding is applied to months and days of 
the  week  using  sine  and  cosine  transformations,  allowing  gradient-boosted  models  to  capture 
seasonal periodicity natively. 

5.3.2 Categorical Encoding and Experience Bucketing 

Categorical features are handled through the native support of LightGBM and CatBoost, avoiding 
dimensionality explosions associated with one-hot encoding. Pilot flight experience is bucketed 
into ordinal bands, capturing structural risk differences without overfitting to granular hour counts. 

5.3.3 Sentence-BERT Narrative Embeddings 

Free-text  narratives  are  encoded  into  fixed  384-dimensional  vectors  using  the  Sentence-BERT 
model  all-MiniLM-L6-v2.  Embeddings  are  L2  normalized  and  persisted  as  numpy  arrays  to  save 
downstream encoding costs. 

5.3.4 LightGBM Leaf Embeddings for Tabular Fusion 

For the third tier of the category classifier, tabular features are converted into dense categorical 
embeddings  by  extracting  the  leaf  index  from  a  trained  LightGBM  model.  This  dense 
representation is concatenated with the Sentence-BERT embedding to fuel the Fusion Multi-Layer 
Perceptron.  

26 

 
 
 
 
5.4 SEVERITY TRIAGE MODELLING METHODOLOGY (PROBLEM A) 

The  severity  triage  module  represents  the  most  methodologically  advanced  component  of  the 
platform. The final architecture integrates multi-modal features with an ordinal cumulative logit 
framework and temperature scaling to deliver cost-sensitive risk assessments.  

5.4.1 Model Selection: Multi-Modal Augmented Gradient Boosting 

The baseline CatBoost model, using only tabular metadata, achieved a Quadratic Weighted Kappa 
(QWK)  of  0.2387.  To  bridge  the  significant  information  gap  inherent  in  structured  fields,  the 
deployed  architecture  utilizes  a  multi-modal  feature  vector.  This  combines  15+  whitelisted 
metadata fields with 32-dimensional, PCA-compressed Sentence-BERT embeddings derived from 
incident narratives.  

An Ordinal LightGBM model was selected for deployment over multinomial competitors. This 
choice  resulted  in  a  93%  relative  improvement  in  QWK  (rising  to  0.4612)  and  a  37%  relative 
improvement  in  macro-F1  (reaching  0.4783).  Narrative  embeddings  were  the  primary  driver, 
closing approximately 50–60% of the information gap identified in the baseline.  

5.4.2 Ordinal Cumulative Logit Decomposition 

Standard multinomial classifiers treat all misclassifications equally, failing to reflect the graded 
nature of aviation severity (Levels 0–3). To align with the quadratic penalty structure of the QWK 
metric,  the  module  reformulates  triage  as  three  binary  tasks.  The  model  predicts  cumulative 
threshold probabilities (e.g., probability that severity is $\geq$ Level 1, $\geq$ Level 2, etc.). Final 
class probabilities are recovered via differencing, providing more robust gradient signals for rare, 
high-severity events.  

5.4.3 Hyperparameter Tuning with Optuna 

The ensemble was optimized using Optuna over 200 trials, a deliberate increase from the baseline 
to accommodate the higher-dimensional multi-modal feature space. The search space prioritized 
validation  QWK,  tuning  parameters  such  as  leaf  count  (15–127),  tree  depth  (4–12),  and 
regularization (10-4 to 101).  

5.4.4 Calibration: Temperature Scaling 

To avoid the overfitting common with isotonic regression on small, rare-event validation sets, the 
system employs temperature scaling. A single scalar parameter τ is fitted by minimizing negative 
log-likelihood.  This  statistical  approach  generalizes  more  reliably  than  non-parametric  curves, 
resulting in a superior test-set Expected Calibration Error (ECE) of 0.0162.  

5.4.5 Cost-Sensitive Prediction

are made by minimizing expected cost across a matrix where missing a critical event is penalized

5.5 CATEGORY CLASSIFICATION METHODOLOGY (PROBLEM B) 

5.5.1 Three-Tier Training Approach 

The three-tier architecture allows for transparent performance attribution: 

1.  Tier 1: One-vs-rest logistic regression on TF-IDF vectors. 
2.  Tier 2: One-vs-rest logistic regression on precomputed Sentence-BERT embeddings. 
3.  Tier 3: Multi-label PyTorch Multi-Layer Perceptron with GELU activations, 

BatchNorm, and dropout regularisation, trained on the concatenation of Sentence-BERT 
embeddings and LightGBM leaf embeddings under a cosine learning rate schedule with 
early stopping (patience 5 epochs). 

The Fusion MLP is trained with focal loss (𝛾 = 2.0) rather than standard binary cross-entropy. 
Focal loss down-weights the easy majority-negative examples by a factor of (1 − 𝑝)𝛾, which 
directs proportionally more gradient signal toward the rare positive examples that drive macro-
F1 performance on imbalanced labels. 

5.5.2 Class Imbalance Handling 

The five safety  categories have substantially different  base  rates. Three additional mechanisms 
address this imbalance at the loss level rather than relying on threshold tuning alone. Per-label 
pos_weight  is  computed  per  category  as  𝑁neg/𝑁poson  the  training  set,  applying  that  ratio  as  a 
multiplicative  weight  to  the  positive-class  term  in  the  focal  loss.  Label  smoothing  with  𝜖 =
0.05replaces hard target labels with the slightly softened values 1 − 𝜖and 𝜖, preventing the model 
from  collapsing  to  near-zero  probabilities  for  rare  labels  and  producing  a  small  but  consistent 
calibration improvement that compounds with the focal loss gain. 

5.5.3 Per-Label Threshold Tuning 

Per-label thresholds are tuned on the validation set by sweeping from 0.05 to 0.95 to maximise 
per-category F1 scores individually. Because the loss function already addresses class imbalance 
through focal loss and per-label positive weighting, threshold tuning operates as a final calibration 
step on a model whose imbalance has been substantially handled at training time, rather than as 
the sole mechanism for managing rare-category performance.

5.6 PRE-FLIGHT RISK MODELLING METHODOLOGY (PROBLEM C) 

5.6.1 Case-Control Sampling Design 

Because incidents occur in fewer than 5% of flights, naïve classifiers fail. A case-control design 
matches NTSB incident records to non-incident BTS flights by route and month, artificially raising 
the positive rate to provide sufficient gradient signal during training.  

5.6.2 Prior Shift Correction 

To  recover  deployment-ready  probabilities  reflecting  true  population  base  rates,  a  closed-form 
prior  shift  odds  adjustment  is  applied.  This  shifts  the  intercept  of  the  underlying  logistic 
relationship while leaving slopes invariant. Isotonic calibration is then applied as a final pass. 

5.7 EMERGING RISK DISCOVERY METHODOLOGY (PROBLEM D) 

5.7.1 Topic Modelling with BERTopic 

SBERT  embeddings  are  reduced 
to  15  dimensions  via  PCA  and  clustered  using 
MiniBatchKMeans. The BERTopic framework merges these using class-based TF-IDF to produce 
30 human-readable topics. PCA and KMeans serve as a graceful fallback for environments lacking 
Numba dependencies required by UMAP/HDBSCAN without compromising downstream logic.  

5.7.2 Severity-Weighted Risk Scoring 

A risk score is computed by multiplying a topic's temporal growth rate, its mean severity (averaged 
supervised labels), and a changepoint multiplier. This ranks topics so analysts are immediately 
alerted to severe, rapidly growing themes. 

5.7.3 PELT Changepoint Detection 

The Pruned Exact Linear Time (PELT) algorithm detects structural statistical shifts in a topic's 
monthly time series. A penalty parameter of three produces confident, sparse changepoints without 
flagging routine monthly noise. 

5.8 CONTRIBUTING FACTOR GRAPH METHODOLOGY (PROBLEM E) 

5.8.1 Heterogeneous Co-occurrence Graph Construction 

Nodes represent  contributing factors, flight phases, aircraft families, and FAR parts. Edges are 
weighted by co-occurrence frequency within incident reports and store the mean severity of the 
contributing incidents. 

5.8.2 Louvain Community Detection 

Louvain community detection partitions the graph by maximizing modularity, identifying clusters 
of related risk factors (e.g., helicopter operations clustering closely with landing phases). 

29 

 
 
 
 
 
5.8.3 Betweenness Centrality and Bridge Factor Identification 

Betweenness centrality identifies bridge factors connecting separate clusters of risk, representing 
critical leverage points for systemic safety interventions. 

5.9 EVALUATION METRICS METHODOLOGY 

•  5.9.1  Quadratic  Weighted  Kappa  (QWK):  The  primary  metric  for  severity  triage.  It 
penalizes misclassifications by the squared distance between predicted and actual classes, 
heavily penalizing large ordinal jumps. 

•  5.9.2 Macro F1: The primary metric for multi-label category classification, weighting all 

categories equally regardless of base rate. 

•  5.9.3  Expected  Calibration  Error  (ECE):  Measures  the  average  absolute  difference 
between  predicted  probabilities  and  empirical  positive  rates  across  bins,  crucial  for 
evaluating threshold readiness. 

•  5.9.4 ROC-AUC and PR-AUC: Used for pre-flight risk to assess ranking ability and the 

operational precision-recall tradeoff in rare-event settings.

6. PROJECT IMPLEMENTATION 

6.1 DEVELOPMENT ENVIRONMENT AND TOOLS 

The system was developed on a combination of Linux and Windows workstations, utilizing Python 
as  the  primary  language  across  both  the  offline  data  pipeline  and  the  online  inference  service. 
Version  control  was  managed  through  Git  via  a  feature-branch  workflow:  every  change  was

merged into the main branch only after passing the test suite and static-analysis quality gates. All 
required  libraries  were  pinned  to  specific  versions  in  a  single  dependency  specification  file  to

The  core  development  tooling  included  Visual  Studio  Code  as  the  primary  editor,  the  pytest

notebooks for exploratory data analysis and rapid iteration on individual ML problems. Docker 
was utilized for containerized packaging. During local development, the Streamlit dashboard was 
executed natively, while the FastAPI inference service was run through Uvicorn. Dependencies 
were  managed  through  standard  Python  packaging  tools.  Persistent  storage  of  trained  model 
artifacts  and processed data utilized the local filesystem during development, transitioning to a 
mounted volume in the deployed configuration.

6.4.1 Containerization 

The  system  is  packaged  as  Docker  containers  to  guarantee  portable  deployment  across 
environments. The FastAPI service and Streamlit dashboard operate in separate containers based 
on slim Python images. Trained model artifacts and processed data files are mounted as shared

6.4.2 Continuous Integration with Jenkins 

A Jenkins pipeline triggers on every source code commit. It executes a clean checkout, installs 
dependencies, runs the 86-test suite via pytest, and aggregates the results. A test failure aborts the

analysis,  builds  the  Docker  images,  tags  them  with  semantic  version  labels,  pushes  them  to  a 
container registry, and notifies the deployment orchestrator.  

6.4.3 Code Quality Enforcement with SonarQube 

SonarQube provides continuous static analysis against strict quality gates. This encompasses code 
style  consistency,  cyclomatic  complexity  limits  (prompting  refactoring  of  overly  complex

methodological  invariants—such  as  leakage  prevention  and  temporal  split  integrity—are 
preserved structurally over the project's lifetime.  

6.4.4 Deployment Topology 

In production, the dashboard and inference containers reside on a single application server with 
sufficient memory to hold the artifacts loaded at startup. Artifacts and processed data reside on a 
persistent disk volume. The CI/CD servers (Jenkins and SonarQube) run on separate infrastructure. 
For  security,  CORS  on  the  FastAPI  service  strictly  permits  only  the  dashboard  origin,  and  all 
external  analyst  communication  utilizes  standard  HTTPS  with  appropriate  certificate

7.2.2 The Information-Gap Argument 

The deployed model's QWK of 0.4612 represents a substantial improvement over the prior tabular-
only baseline of 0.2387, reported in earlier iterations of the platform when the severity classifier 
consumed only pre-circumstance structured fields. This earlier baseline was previously interpreted 

40 

 
 
 
 
as the ceiling on what could be predicted from pre-circumstance metadata alone under leakage-
strict feature audit. The current result revises this interpretation. 

The substantive change  between the two models is  the addition  of PCA-compressed Sentence-
BERT embeddings of the report narrative and synopsis fields as input features. The narratives are 
written by the reporting pilot or controller before the formal incident outcome fields are populated, 
which means they remain admissible under the strict pre-circumstance feature audit. They contain, 
however, dense descriptive content about how the incident developed from the reporter's first-hand 
observation, which is content that the structured tabular fields do not capture in any form. 

Empirically, the inclusion of these narrative embeddings closes approximately 50 to 60 percent of 
the information gap that previously separated the leakage-strict tabular baseline (QWK 0.2387) 
from  the  implied  performance  of  models  that  include  post-incident  outcome  fields.  The 
methodological claim of the project therefore changes character. Rather than arguing that severity 
prediction without post-incident features is structurally limited to modest performance, the project 
now demonstrates that meaningful predictive signal lies in the pre-circumstance narrative content 
itself,  signal  that  can  be  extracted  under  the  same  leakage  discipline  that  excluded  structured 
outcome fields. The prior leakage-safe ceiling was an artefact of feature representation rather than 
a fundamental limit on the problem. 

7.2.3 Calibration Quality 

The Expected Calibration Error of 0.0162 for the deployed model indicates that the temperature-
scaled  probabilities  match  empirical  positive  rates  closely.  Across  the  four  severity  levels,  the 
average absolute deviation between the predicted probability bin centres and the empirical positive 
rate  within  those  bins  is  approximately  one  and  a  half  percentage  points.  This  is  a  substantial 
improvement  over  the  0.028  ECE  produced  by  per-class  isotonic  regression  on  the  equivalent 
baseline, and reflects the better generalisation behaviour of single-parameter temperature scaling 
on a validation set whose rare-class bins have limited support. The improved calibration directly 
benefits  the  cost-sensitive  prediction  stage:  the  expected  cost  computation  is  sensitive  to 
calibration error in the rare critical class, and ECE values in the 0.016 range produce stable cost-
sensitive class assignments rather than volatility driven by miscalibrated tail probabilities. 

7.2.4 Cost-Sensitive Prediction in Operation 

The cost-sensitive prediction shifts the predicted class distribution relative to the argmax of the 
calibrated probabilities. On the test set, the cost-sensitive predictions show a higher rate of upward 
triage  relative  to  argmax,  particularly  for  borderline  cases  that  fall  between  adjacent  severity 
levels. Borderline level two cases, for instance, are pushed up to level three more often under cost-
sensitive prediction than under argmax, reflecting the asymmetric cost of misclassifying a critical 
incident as substantial. This is the operationally correct behaviour for a safety analytics platform, 
and analyst review of the cost-sensitive vs argmax disagreements during validation confirmed that 
the cost-sensitive choice was preferable in the cases reviewed.

7.3.2 Per-Label Performance Variation 

The macro F1 averages over the five categories with equal weight, but the per category F1 values 
are not uniform. Categories with higher base rates (Flight Operations and Equipment and Systems) 
42 

 
 
 
 
achieve higher per-category F1, while categories with lower base rates (Airspace and Navigation) 
achieve lower per-category F1. This  is  the expected pattern for multi-label  classification under 
imbalanced label distributions. The new training discipline reduces but does not eliminate this gap. 
Focal loss with 𝛾 = 2.0down-weights easy majority-class negatives, which directs more gradient 
signal  toward  rare-category  positives  during  training.  Per-label  pos_weight  derived  from

0.05prevents the model from collapsing to near-zero probabilities for rare labels. The combined 
effect is a per-category F1 spread that is meaningfully tighter than under standard binary cross-
entropy training, although it is not eliminated entirely; rare-category prediction remains the harder 
of the five sub-problems within Problem B.

analyst  reviewing  the  predicted  categories  for  a  given  incident  report  can  expect  the  model  to 
identify the principal categories reliably, with occasional missed categories or extraneous category 
assignments concentrated in the rarer categories. The platform's framing of the model as a decision 
support tool rather than a final automated decision maker remains the correct positioning at this

7.4.2 Threshold Sweep and Operational Tuning 

The  deployment  threshold  was  tuned  through  a  200-step  sweep  over  the  calibrated  probability 
range. The conservative heuristic threshold of approximately five times the true population prior, 
used during initial development, produces an F1 of 0.2287 on the test set. The tuned threshold of 
approximately  4.2  times  the  true  prior  produces  an  F1  of  0.3104,  an  absolute  improvement  of 
0.0817 (relative improvement approximately 36 percent). The shape of the F1 curve across the 
swept threshold range is approximately concave with a clearly identifiable peak, which suggests 
that the operational threshold is robust to small variations in the deployment population and that 
the tuning is not overfitting to a single point in a noisy region of the curve. 

7.4.3 Validation vs Test Performance Discrepancy 

A  methodologically  important  observation  concerns  the  gap  between  validation  performance 
computed on the case-control matched dataset and test performance computed on the temporal test 
split.  The  case-control  validation  set,  by  construction,  contains  negatives  that  are  matched  to 
positives on route and month covariates, which means the validation classifier sees a much harder 
discrimination problem than the deployment classifier ever faces. Validation metrics on this set 

44 

 
 
 
 
therefore  overstate  true  deployment  performance  by  a  margin  proportional  to  the  matching 
strength. 

The temporal test split, in contrast, draws negatives at random from the unmatched population. 
The reported ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, and F1 figures reflect performance against this honest test set 
and are therefore the values an analyst should expect when the model is  deployed against new 
flights  drawn  from  the  operational  population.  This  methodological  choice  produces  lower 
headline numbers than the case-control validation set would have produced, but the lower numbers 
are the trustworthy ones, consistent with the broader methodological discipline of the project: it is 
preferable to report a defensible number than a flattering one. 

7.4.4 Operational Interpretation 

The  deployed  pre-flight  risk  model  produces  calibrated,  ranked  risk  scores  suitable  for 
incorporation  into  airline  operational  risk  review  workflows.  The  tuned  operating  point  at  F1 
0.3104 reflects the rare-event nature of aviation incidents: precision of any single high-risk flag is 
necessarily limited by the underlying base rate, but the rank ordering of flagged flights preserves 
substantial information value for prioritising analyst attention. The platform's framing of pre-flight 
risk  scoring  as  a  triage  and  prioritisation  aid,  rather  than  a  flight-disposition  decision  maker, 
matches this performance regime. Analysts using the model can expect a meaningful enrichment 
of  high-risk  flights  in  the  top-ranked  output  relative  to  random  sampling,  while  remaining 
responsible for the operational decisions about which flagged flights warrant follow-up action.

7.7 SYSTEM PERFORMANCE RESULTS 

Basic operations such as loading the dashboard and navigating between problem pages complete 
within a fraction of a second. The FastAPI inference service starts up in under five seconds when 
all  model  artifacts  are  present,  with  the  loading  time  dominated  by  the  deserialization  of  the 
Ordinal LightGBM ensemble and the Sentence-BERT encoder. Once started, the service processes 
inference requests for severity, category, and pre-flight scoring within tens to  low hundreds of 
milliseconds,  well  within  the  sub-second  target  established  in  the  non-functional  requirements. 
The  corpus-level  analytic  outputs  for  emerging-risk  discovery  and  the  factor  graph  are 
precomputed by the offline pipeline and cached as filesystem artifacts. The dashboard reads these 
artifacts from the model store on demand, keeping the user interface highly responsive despite the 
computational expense of the underlying analytical pipelines. The full offline pipeline, from raw 
data ingestion through to the generation of all five problem artifacts, completes in tens of minutes 
on a single CPU machine, easily accommodating a daily or weekly retraining cadence. Memory 
consumption of the running inference service is  governed by the loaded model artifacts, fitting 
comfortably  within  eight  gigabytes  of  RAM,  while  CPU  utilization  remains  low  to  moderate 
during inference, demonstrating that no GPU acceleration is required for analyst-level interactive 
use. 

7.8 SEMANTIC RETRIEVAL AND DIVERSITY DISCUSSION 

The architecture does not include a retrieval-augmented generation component in the sense of a 
large language model grounded in retrieved documents. Instead, the semantic retrieval within the 
system is strictly bounded to the topic assignment phase of the emerging-risk discovery pipeline 
and the cosine-based topic representations within the BERTopic framework. The relevance and 
diversity of the surfaced topics are therefore a direct property of the clustering pipeline rather than 
a separate generative retrieval layer. Validation runs comparing topics surfaced across multiple 
offline  pipeline  executions  on  the  same  input  data  confirmed  that  topic  assignment  is  fully 
deterministic  based  on  the  global  random  seed,  producing  identical  topics  across  runs. 
Furthermore,  comparisons  of  the  topics  surfaced  using  the  Principal  Component  Analysis  and 
MiniBatchKMeans  backend  against  a  development  run  utilizing  UMAP  and  HDBSCAN 
confirmed that  the top-ranked topics by risk score remain largely  consistent. This  supports the 
conclusion that the operational outputs of the emerging-risk discovery capability are robust to the 
choice of clustering backend, and that the chosen fallback path does not materially degrade the 
analytical value or thematic diversity of the surfaced topics.

7.9.1 Strengths of the Implemented System 

The results confirm that the system successfully achieves its core objectives. The integration of 
five distinct machine learning problems behind a single dashboard, backed by a unified inference 
service and a shared offline pipeline, is realized in robust working software with persisted artifacts 
and reproducible outputs. The methodological discipline running through the project—the strict 
feature whitelist, the temporal split, cost-sensitive prediction, temperature-scaled calibration, and

LightGBM ensemble achieved a Quadratic Weighted Kappa of 0.4612, a Macro-F1 of 0.4783, and 
an Expected Calibration Error of 0.0162. This effectively doubled the performance of the tabular-

7.9.2 Limitations and Honest Observations 

Several  limitations  of  the  system  deserve  direct  acknowledgment.  While  the  severity  triage 
Quadratic Weighted Kappa of 0.4612 represents a massive leap over the baseline, it functions best 
as  an  ordering  and  triage  signal  rather  than  a  flawless  per-incident  oracle.  Analyst  review  of 
borderline cases remains necessary, reinforcing the platform's positioning as a decision-support 
tool  rather  than  an  automated  decision-maker.  For  category  classification,  a  Hamming  Loss  of 
0.0712 indicates high overall accuracy, but extremely rare safety categories still present edge-case 
classification challenges that require human oversight. Additionally, the emerging-risk discovery 
pipeline  and  factor  graph  generation  are  not  currently  parameterized  for  real-time  streaming 
updates as new incident reports arrive. The pipeline is designed for batch retraining on the full 
corpus, setting the operational tempo to a daily or weekly cadence rather than minute-by-minute 
live monitoring. Finally, the contributing-factor graph operates exclusively on the structured fields 
of the corpus; expanding this capability to extract latent causal factors directly from the free-text 
narratives via named entity recognition remains an open avenue for future enhancement. 

7.9.3 Comparison with Existing Systems 

The  proposed  system  is  best  evaluated  against  the  broader  landscape  of  public  aviation  safety 
analytics  tooling  rather  than  any  single  competing  platform.  Commercial  Flight  Operations 
Quality Assurance software remains the operational standard for airline  safety teams, yet these 
systems are almost entirely rule-based. They detect only what they are programmed to detect and 
cannot  surface  emerging  thematic  patterns  or  compute  calibrated  risk  probabilities  from 
heterogeneous  narrative  and  tabular  data.  The  proposed  platform  complements  these  tools  by 
targeting  the  proactive  analyst  workflow  rather  than  routine  exceedance  review.  Compared  to 
open-source text-mining scripts, this system integrates structured flight telemetry and computes 
calibrated severity probabilities, producing cross-problem analytical outputs that isolated scripts 
cannot  match.  Furthermore,  while  generic  large  language  models  can  answer  general  aviation

significant  differentiator  between  this  platform  and  comparable  academic  work  is  its  strict

achieved without compromising the structural integrity of the temporal and leakage audits.

8.1 CONCLUSION 

This  project  presented  an  AI-driven  safety  analytics  platform  designed  to  shift  flight  data 
monitoring from a reactive process to a proactive one. It addresses a critical gap in the public 
aviation  safety  landscape  by  successfully  integrating  five  distinct  machine  learning 
capabilities  into  a  single,  dashboard-driven  decision-support  tool,  backed  by  a  unified 
inference  service  and  a  shared  offline  pipeline.  The  platform's  architecture  tackles  each 
problem utilizing advanced, tailored methodologies. The severity triage capability utilizes an 
Ordinal LightGBM ensemble combining tabular metadata with PCA-compressed Sentence-
BERT narratives, applying cumulative logit decomposition, temperature scaling, and a 100× 
asymmetric  cost  penalty  for  fatal  misclassifications.  The  category  classification  capability 
employs a three-tier architecture culminating in a Fusion Multi-Layer Perceptron trained with 
focal  loss,  label  smoothing,  per-label  positive-class  weighting,  and  a  cosine  learning  rate 
schedule. The pre-flight risk model is built on a case-control sampling design utilizing prior-
shift correction, isotonic calibration, and an operational threshold sweep. The emerging-risk 
discovery pipeline combines BERTopic clustering with severity-weighted risk scoring and 
PELT  changepoint  detection.  Finally,  the  factor  graph  analysis  constructs  a  60-node 
heterogeneous  co-occurrence  network,  applying  Louvain  community  detection  and 
betweenness centrality to identify bridge factors. 

The  project’s  principal  differentiator  is  its  rigorous  methodological  discipline.  A  strict, 
audited feature whitelist and temporal  data splitting prevent  both  post-incident  and future-
data  leakage.  Post-training  calibration  ensures  the  generation  of  deployment-ready 
probabilities,  while  deterministic  seeds  guarantee  reproducibility.  A  robust  continuous 
integration pipeline, enforced through Jenkins and SonarQube, ensures these methodological 
invariants are preserved at every code commit. The entire system is containerized, utilizing a 
FastAPI and GraphQL backend that loads artifacts into memory at startup to provide low-
latency, CPU-efficient interactive analysis via a Streamlit dashboard. 

Evaluation under strict temporal validation yielded substantial results, fully mapped to the 
locked metrics. The deployed Ordinal LightGBM achieved a Quadratic Weighted Kappa of 
0.4612, a Macro-F1 of 0.4783, and an Expected Calibration Error of 0.0162. The inclusion of 
pre-circumstance narrative embeddings nearly doubled the performance of the tabular-only 
CatBoost baseline (QWK 0.2387, Macro-F1 0.3488, ECE 0.028), proving that prior leakage-
strict performance ceilings were merely artifacts of feature representation. The Fusion MLP 
achieved  a  Macro-F1  of  0.7038,  a  Micro-F1  of  0.7782,  and  a  Hamming  Loss  of  0.0712, 
successfully overcoming extreme class imbalances. The pre-flight risk model established a 
defensible baseline for rare-event prediction, achieving an ROC-AUC of 0.7641 and a PR-
AUC of 0.1843, with an F1 score of 0.3104 at the optimally tuned threshold of 4.2 times the 
true  prior.  The  unsupervised  discovery  pipeline  surfaced  30  narrative  topics  alongside  13 
detected changepoints, flagging critical shifts in tire, gear, and smoke events, and generated 
high-severity  edge patterns  that align strongly  with  established aviation  safety  knowledge. 
Ultimately, the system demonstrates that integrating rigorous machine learning with public 
aviation  data  provides  highly  actionable  decision  support.  It  does  not  aim  to  replace  the 
trained  judgment  of  human  safety  analysts,  but  rather  amplifies  it  by  surfacing  complex, 
trustworthy patterns at scale within a single coherent workflow.

8.2 FURTHER ENHANCEMENTS 

8.2.1 Narrative-Based Contributing-Factor Extraction 

The current contributing-factor graph operates strictly on the structured fields of the ASRS 
corpus and does  not  extract  factors from  the  free-text narratives. A natural  extension  is  to 
apply  Named  Entity  Recognition  (NER)—trained  or  fine-tuned  on  aviation  safety 
vocabulary—to  surface  contributing  factors  mentioned  in  the  narratives  but  omitted  from 
structured  assessment  fields.  These  extracted  factors  would  feed  into  the  existing 
heterogeneous  co-occurrence  graph,  enriching  the  node  and  edge  structure  and  surfacing 
patterns currently invisible to structured-field analysis. 

8.2.2 Real-Time and Streaming Operation 

The current system operates as a batch analytics platform utilizing  an offline pipeline that 
retrains models on the full corpus on demand. An operational enhancement would support 
incremental updates as new incident reports arrive, allowing the emerging-risk and factor-
graph  capabilities  to  respond  to  data  with  minute-level,  rather  than  weekly,  latency.  This 
requires streaming incoming reports into the embedding generation pipeline, assigning new 
reports to existing topics, updating topic-count time series, re-running changepoint detection 
on a rolling window, and dynamically updating graph edges. The architectural foundation for 
this transition is already in place, as the offline pipeline is deliberately decoupled from the 
inference service. 

8.2.3 Multimodal Document Support 

Currently, the ingestion pipeline processes the structured fields and textual narratives of the 
ASRS corpus. Expanding the system to ingest additional safety documents—including NTSB 
incident investigation reports, FAA safety bulletins, and operator safety advisories—would 
significantly  broaden  its  analytic  scope.  Each  document  class  would  require  a  custom 
ingestion adapter and targeted engineering rules. The established Sentence-BERT embedding 
pipeline would generalize naturally to these additional document types. 

8.2.4 Fine-Tuned Domain-Specific Embeddings 

The current implementation utilizes the general-purpose Sentence-BERT model (all-MiniLM-
L6-v2)  for  narrative  embedding.  Future  enhancements  should  involve  fine-tuning  the 
embedding  model  on  aviation  safety  vocabulary  using  the  ASRS  corpus  itself.  Consistent 
with  recent  prior  art  highlighting  the  superiority  of  domain-adapted  models  like  Aviation-
BERT, these fine-tuned embeddings would better capture the domain-specific semantics of 
aviation incidents, driving improved category classification performance and generating more 
semantically coherent topic clusters. 

8.2.5 Active Learning for Severity Rubric Refinement 

The  current  severity  rubric  is  a  deterministic  function  of  post-incident  outcome  fields, 
versioned  in  a  YAML  file  and  evolved  through  deliberate  review  by  domain  experts.  A 
valuable enhancement would be the introduction of an active learning loop. Incidents where 
the  rubric-assigned  severity  differs  drastically  from  human  analyst  reviews  could  be 
automatically  flagged.  This  would  close  the  loop  between  the  analyst  experience  and  the 

52 

 
 
rubric definition, allowing safety standards to evolve based on real-world operational edge 
cases rather than solely on a priori regulatory definitions. 

8.2.6 Explainability Through SHAP 

Currently, the platform reports global feature importance from the gradient-boosted models. 
Adding per-prediction explainability through SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values 
would allow analysts to see the precise contribution of each individual feature to a specific 
severity prediction or pre-flight risk score. As demonstrated in recent interpretable machine 
learning frameworks for aviation data, this is particularly valuable for borderline predictions 
where the cost-sensitive choice differs from the argmax, helping analysts understand exactly 
why the model favored the cost-sensitive class. 

8.2.7 Plagiarism and Authenticity Verification for Voluntary Reports 

The voluntary nature of the ASRS corpus is its principal strength, but it introduces a latent 
concern regarding data authenticity; a subset of reports may be templated or copied. Applying 
text similarity analysis across the corpus to flag reports that are suspiciously identical to prior 
submissions would surface data provenance issues for analyst review. The existing Sentence-
BERT  embedding  pipeline  easily  supports  this  via  corpus-wide  cosine  similarity 
computations. 

8.2.8 Multi-Tenancy and Operator-Specific Models 

The current implementation produces a single set of models trained on the combined corpus 
across  all  operators  represented  in  ASRS.  For  deployment  within  a  specific  airline  or 
regulatory body, the platform should support operator-specific model variants. These variants 
would fine-tune the base model using the operator's internal safety data. Implementing this 
requires  a  multi-tenancy  layer  within  the  inference  service  and  a  robust  model-versioning 
scheme to distinguish the public base model from proprietary overlays. 

8.2.9 Integration with Operational FOQA Systems 

The platform is positioned as a complementary tool to existing rule-based Flight Operations 
Quality  Assurance  (FOQA)  software  rather  than  a  direct  replacement.  A  powerful  future 
integration would consume FOQA exceedance flags as additional predictive features in the 
severity  triage  and  pre-flight  risk  models,  allowing  the  AI-driven  analytics  to  incorporate 
operational signals FOQA already successfully captures. Conversely, the severity-weighted 
risk scores and factor-graph outputs generated by this platform could feed directly back into 
FOQA workflows, allowing rule-writers to prioritize new telemetry exceedance parameters 
based on the emerging patterns surfaced by the machine learning suite.

A.4 COST-SENSITIVE PREDICTION ALGORITHM 

The cost sensitive prediction stage of the severity triage pipeline computes the predicted class as the 
class that minimises the expected cost under the configured cost matrix. The expected cost vector for 
each input is computed as the matrix product of the calibrated probability vector and the cost matrix. 
The predicted class is the index of the minimum element of the expected cost vector. 

python 

import numpy as np 
def build_cost_matrix(cost_config: dict, num_classes: int = 4) -> np.ndarray: 
    C = np.zeros((num_classes, num_classes), dtype=float) 
    for i in range(num_classes): 
        row = cost_config.get(f"actual_{i}", {}) 
        for j in range(num_classes): 
            C[i, j] = row.get(f"pred_{j}", 0.0) 
    return C 
def predict_cost_sensitive(probs: np.ndarray, 
                           cost_config: dict, 
                           num_classes: int = 4) -> np.ndarray: 
    """ 
    Choose the prediction that minimises expected cost for each sample. 
        E[cost | pred=j] = sum_i P(actual=i) * C[i, j] 
    Parameters 
    ---------- 
    probs : np.ndarray, shape (n_samples, num_classes) 
        Calibrated class probabilities. 
    cost_config : dict 
        The costs sub-dict from cost_matrix.yaml. 
    Returns 
    ------- 
    np.ndarray of shape (n_samples,) — integer class predictions. 
    """ 
    C = build_cost_matrix(cost_config, num_classes) 
    expected_cost = probs @ C        # (n_samples, num_classes) 
    return expected_cost.argmin(axis=1)

A.5 PRIOR SHIFT CORRECTION FOR PRE-FLIGHT RISK 

The prior shift correction applied to the pre flight risk model recovers deployment ready probabilities 
from the case control sampled training distribution. The closed form odds adjustment converts the 
sampled probability to sampled odds, multiplies by the ratio of true prior odds to training prior odds, 
and converts back to a probability. 

61 

 
 
python 

import numpy as np 
def adjust_prior(p_sampled, p_true_prior, p_train_prior): 
    p_sampled = np.clip(p_sampled, 1e-15, 1 - 1e-15) 
    odds_sampled = p_sampled / (1.0 - p_sampled) 
    odds_true_prior = p_true_prior / (1.0 - p_true_prior) 
    odds_train_prior = p_train_prior / (1.0 - p_train_prior) 
    odds_adjusted = odds_sampled * (odds_true_prior / odds_train_prior) 
    return odds_adjusted / (1.0 + odds_adjusted) 
class PriorShiftedCalibratedModel: 
    """ 
    Wraps a calibrated classifier to apply prior shift correction at 
    prediction time. Used when the underlying model was trained on a 
    case-control sampled dataset whose positive rate differs from the 
    deployment-time true rate. 
    """ 
    def __init__(self, calibrated_model, true_prior, train_prior, features): 
        self.calibrated_model = calibrated_model 
        self.true_prior = true_prior 
        self.train_prior = train_prior 
        self.features = features 

    def predict_proba(self, X): 
        probs = self.calibrated_model.predict_proba(X) 
        p_sampled = probs[:, 1] 
        p_adjusted = adjust_prior(p_sampled, self.true_prior, self.train_prior) 
        return np.vstack([1 - p_adjusted, p_adjusted]).T 

    def predict(self, X, threshold=None): 
        probs = self.predict_proba(X)[:, 1] 
        if threshold is None: 
            threshold = self.true_prior * 5 
        return (probs >= threshold).astype(int)
